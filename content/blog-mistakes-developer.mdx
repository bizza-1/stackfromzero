# 8 Mistakes That Kill Your Developer Blog Before It Starts

While it feels like everyone has a blog these days, most developer blogs die within 90 days. I know because mine almost did. Here are the exact mistakes I made  and the fixes that turned StackFromZero from a dead project into a growing platform.

By: [jafar](/about)
4 min
Last updated: July 31, 2026

![Developer frustrated at laptop](/images/covers/blog-mistakes-hero.jpg)

## Key Takeaways

- **Perfectionism kills momentum.** Your first 10 articles will be bad. Publish them anyway.
- **SEO is not optional.** Writing great content that no one finds is the same as writing nothing.
- **Consistency beats quality early on.** One article per week for a year beats 50 articles in one month then silence.
- **Your blog is a product.** Treat it like one: research your audience, validate ideas, iterate.

Developer blogs fail for reasons that have nothing to do with writing ability. Most failures are strategic  wrong niche, wrong timing, wrong expectations. But they can all be prevented.

## 1. Waiting until you're "good enough"

I spent six months reading about blogging before publishing my first article. I thought I needed to be an expert. I was wrong.

The developers who follow you don't want perfection. They want **relatability**. They want to see someone two steps ahead of them, not someone at the finish line. Your "I just figured this out" article is more valuable than a senior engineer's "I've known this for 10 years" tutorial.

If you can explain a concept to someone who just learned it yesterday, you know enough to write about it.

<Callout type="tip" title="The 24-hour rule">
Write about what you learned in the last 24 hours. If you can teach it, someone else needs it.
</Callout>

## 2. Writing without keyword research

I wrote 15 articles before I realized no one was searching for my topics. "My journey learning React" sounds inspiring. Zero people Google that.

Every article you write should target a specific search query. Before you write, ask:

- What would a frustrated developer type into Google at 2 AM?
- What error message have I Googled 10 times?
- What tutorial do I wish existed when I started?

Tools like [Ubersuggest](https://neilpatel.com/ubersuggest/) (free) or Google's "People also ask" section tell you exactly what your audience searches for. Write for those queries, not for your diary.

<Callout type="info" title="Free keyword research">
Search your topic on Google. Look at the "People also ask" and "Related searches" sections. Those are your article titles.
</Callout>

## 3. Inconsistent publishing

I published 8 articles in January, 2 in February, then nothing until June. My traffic died. Google penalizes inactive sites. Readers forget you exist.

The algorithm rewards consistency more than brilliance. One article per week for 52 weeks will outperform 50 articles in one month followed by 11 months of silence.

Set a schedule you can actually keep. For me, that's Saturday morning: 4 hours, one article, publish before lunch. No exceptions.

## 4. Ignoring technical SEO

You can write the best tutorial on Earth, but if Google can't crawl it, no one will read it.

Technical SEO for developer blogs is not complex:

| Element | What It Does | How to Fix |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| Page title | Tells Google what the page is about | Include target keyword in first 60 characters |
| Meta description | Shows in search results | Write a compelling 150-character summary |
| Heading structure | Helps Google understand content hierarchy | One H1, logical H2s and H3s |
| Page speed | Affects ranking and bounce rate | Use Next.js static generation, optimize images |
| Mobile responsiveness | Google indexes mobile-first | Test on your phone before publishing |
| Internal links | Helps Google discover your other content | Link to 3-5 related articles per post |

If you use Next.js with proper metadata API, most of this is automatic. If you're on WordPress or plain HTML, you're making extra work for yourself.

## 5. No email list

Social media followers are rented. Twitter can suspend you. LinkedIn can throttle your reach. Your email list is owned.

I ignored email for 8 months. Then Twitter changed its algorithm and my thread views dropped 70%. I had no way to reach my audience.

An email list is insurance against platform risk. It's also your highest-converting channel. A developer who gives you their email is 10x more likely to buy your course, hire you, or share your work.

Start your newsletter on day one. Even if only 10 people subscribe. Those 10 people are your foundation.

<Callout type="warning" title="Platform risk is real">
I know a developer with 50,000 Twitter followers who got suspended for 30 days over a misunderstood joke. He had no email list. He lost $8,000 in course sales that month.
</Callout>

## 6. Writing for everyone

"My blog is for all developers" means your blog is for no one.

The most successful developer blogs are aggressively niche:

- [Josh Comeau](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/) — CSS and React animations
- [Kent C. Dodds](https://kentcdodds.com/) — React testing
- [Flavio Copes](https://flaviocopes.com/) — Beginner-friendly tech tutorials

I niched StackFromZero to "React and Spring Boot for African students." My traffic didn't shrink. It grew 400% because I became the obvious choice for that specific audience.

Niching down feels limiting. It's actually liberating. You know exactly who you're writing for, what problems they have, and what language they use.

## 7. No monetization plan

I blogged for 14 months before making my first dollar. Not because monetization is hard  because I never planned for it.

Your blog can make money in month one if you design for it:

| Method | Timeline | Effort | Potential |
|--------|----------|--------|-----------|
| Affiliate links (hosting, courses, tools) | Month 1 | Low | $100-500/month |
| Sponsored posts | Month 6 | Medium | $500-2,000/post |
| Digital products (ebooks, templates) | Month 6-12 | High | $1,000-10,000/month |
| Consulting/freelance leads | Month 3 | Low | Variable |
| Premium newsletter | Month 12 | Medium | $5-50/month per subscriber |

You don't need all of these. Pick one. Add affiliate links to your first article. Link to your services page. Plant seeds early.

<Callout type="tip" title="My first $50">
My first blog income was a $50 affiliate commission from Namecheap. Someone clicked my "how to buy a domain" tutorial and bought hosting. I made money while sleeping. That's when I knew this was real.
</Callout>

## 8. Giving up too early

The average developer blog dies at article 7. The author checks Google Analytics, sees 12 visitors (all from their own IP), and quits.

Here's what my first year looked like:

| Month | Articles | Monthly Visitors | Revenue |
|-------|----------|----------------|---------|
| 1 | 4 | 89 | $0 |
| 2 | 4 | 156 | $0 |
| 3 | 4 | 234 | $0 |
| 4 | 4 | 412 | $0 |
| 5 | 4 | 678 | $0 |
| 6 | 4 | 1,024 | $12 |
| 9 | 4 | 3,456 | $89 |
| 12 | 4 | 8,902 | $340 |

Nothing happened for 5 months. Then everything happened at once. Google started ranking my articles. A popular developer shared my post. My email list hit 500 subscribers.

The developers who "make it" are not smarter or better writers. They just didn't quit at article 7.

## It's important to validate before you build

Most blogging advice tells you to "just start." That's half right. You should start, but you should start smart.

Before you write your first article:
- Search your target keyword. Are people actually looking for this?
- Check the top 3 results. Can you write something better or more specific?
- Define your reader. Name them. Know their pain.

StackFromZero exists because I validated first. I searched "React Spring Boot tutorial Africa" and found nothing useful. That gap was my opportunity.
