# From Lagos to $4,000 a Month: How I Landed My First Remote Developer Job

In January 2025, I was coding on a laptop with a cracked screen in a Lagos bedroom. The power had been out for six hours. My phone hotspot was burning through my last 2GB of data. I had zero professional experience, no computer science degree, and no connections in tech.

In March 2026, I signed a contract for $4,000 a month with a European startup. Fully remote. Paid in dollars. I work from my apartment in Yaba, take meetings at 2 PM because of the timezone overlap, and I have never met my boss in person.

This is exactly how I did it. No shortcuts. No luck. Just a system that anyone can copy.

## Key Takeaways

- **Your location is an advantage, not a handicap.** European companies need developers who can work during their business hours. Lagos is one hour ahead of London.
- **A blog is better than a resume.** My StackFromZero blog got me more interviews than my CV ever did. Companies hired me for what I could demonstrate, not where I studied.
- **GitHub is your portfolio.** Three solid projects beat fifty tutorial completions every time.
- **The first $1,000 is the hardest.** After that, each raise gets easier because you have proof.
- **It took 14 months, not 14 days.** Anyone promising overnight success is lying.

I am not special. I am not a genius. I am just a developer who documented everything he learned, shared it publicly, and refused to quit when the data ran out.

<Callout type="warning" title="The honest timeline">
This took 14 months from my first line of code to my first paycheck. If you need money next week, this guide will not help you. If you can commit to a year of building in public, it will change your life.
</Callout>

## 1. The starting point: worse than you think

Let me be specific about where I started so you understand what is possible.

In January 2025, I had:
- A 2018 HP laptop with 4GB RAM
- A phone hotspot that cost ₦1,500 per GB
- No savings. I was living on ₦20,000 a week from tutoring math.
- Zero coding experience beyond copying HTML from W3Schools
- No LinkedIn profile
- No GitHub account
- No idea what React or Spring Boot were

I discovered coding because I needed a website for my tutoring business. I could not afford to hire someone. So I watched a free Traversy Media tutorial on YouTube and built it myself. It was ugly. It broke on mobile. But it worked.

That ugly website was the first proof that I could build things. It was also the first item in what would become my portfolio.

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with a real problem">
Do not learn to code by following tutorials for their own sake. Build something you actually need. A website for your church. An expense tracker for your family. A portfolio for your side hustle. Real problems create real motivation.
</Callout>

## 2. The learning phase: 8 months of building in public

From January to August 2025, I learned React and Spring Boot. But I did not just learn. I documented every step.

Every time I figured something out, I wrote a blog post about it. Not because I was an expert. Because I was one day ahead of the person who would read it.

My first posts were terrible. Bad grammar. Wrong explanations. Code that would make a senior developer cringe. But I published them anyway.

Here is what happened:

| Jan 2025 | 2 | 15 | 12 | ₦0 |
| Feb 2025 | 3 | 28 | 34 | ₦0 |
| Mar 2025 | 3 | 42 | 67 | ₦0 |
| Apr 2025 | 2 | 35 | 89 | ₦0 |

Eight months. Zero income. But something invisible was happening.

A recruiter from a Kenyan startup found my blog through a Google search. They offered me a freelance gig for $500. I almost cried.

That $500 was not just money. It was proof that someone on the internet would pay me to code.

## 3. The first client: how I got hired without a degree

The Kenyan startup found my article about deploying Spring Boot on Render. They were stuck on the exact same problem. My tutorial solved it for them.

They checked my GitHub. They saw three projects:
- A task manager with React and Spring Boot
- A student expense tracker
- A blog platform (which became StackFromZero)

None of them were original ideas. All of them were built by following tutorials and adding my own features. But they were complete. They worked. They had README files and live demos.

The recruiter sent me a message on LinkedIn. I did not even have a proper LinkedIn profile. I had just created one two months earlier and copy-pasted my blog posts into the featured section.

The interview was a 30-minute video call. They asked me to explain one of my GitHub projects. I talked about the task manager for 20 minutes. The tech lead asked one coding question. I solved it in 10 minutes because I had practiced the exact pattern the week before.

They offered $500 for a two-week project. I accepted in 30 seconds.

<Callout type="info" title="What actually got me hired">
It was not my degree. I do not have one. It was not my experience. I had none. It was the combination of a public blog that proved I could explain things, a GitHub profile that proved I could build things, and a project that solved their exact problem.
</Callout>

## 4. The scaling phase: from $500 to $4,000

After the Kenyan gig, I had three things I did not have before:
1. A testimonial
2. A client reference
3. Confidence that someone would pay me

I used all three to get the next client. And the next. Here is how the income grew:

| Period | Client | Rate | Total Earned | Key Lesson |
|--------|--------|------|--------------|------------|
| Aug 2025 | Kenyan startup | $500 flat | $500 | First proof of paid work |
| Sep 2025 | Nigerian fintech | $15/hour | $1,200 | Hourly billing is risky |
| Nov 2025 | UK agency | $25/hour | $2,400 | European clients pay more |
| Jan 2026 | German startup | $3,500/month | $3,500 | Monthly retainer is stable |
| Mar 2026 | European startup | $4,000/month | $4,000 | Blog traffic = inbound leads |

The jump from $25/hour to $3,500/month happened because of StackFromZero.

A CTO in Berlin found my blog through a Reddit post. He read three articles. He checked my GitHub. He sent me an email that said: "I need someone who can build what you write about. Are you available?"

We had one call. He asked about my Spring Boot deployment guide. I explained my Render setup, my database configuration, my CI/CD pipeline. He said: "When can you start?"

No coding interview. No whiteboard. No degree check. Just proof of work.

## 5. The exact strategy: what I did every day

Here is the system I followed for 14 months. It is not glamorous. It is not fast. But it works.

### Morning: Build (4 hours)

- Code for 2 hours on a real project
- Write for 2 hours about what I learned
- Publish the article the same day or the next

### Afternoon: Share (2 hours)

- Post the article on Twitter with a thread summarizing the key point
- Share on LinkedIn with a personal story about why I wrote it
- Submit to r/reactjs or r/SpringBoot if relevant
- Comment on 3 other developers' posts to build relationships

### Evening: Improve (1 hour)

- Read one technical article or documentation page
- Fix one bug in an old project
- Update my GitHub README if something changed

That is 7 hours a day, 6 days a week, for 14 months. While tutoring math to pay for data. While coding on a laptop that took 5 minutes to open Chrome.

<Callout type="warning" title="The part nobody talks about">
I missed parties. I stopped watching football. I coded through Ramadan fasting and Christmas holidays. My friends thought I was crazy. For 8 months, nothing happened. No money. No followers. No validation. The only thing that kept me going was the habit.
</Callout>

## 6. The platforms that actually work for African developers

Not all job platforms are equal. Here is what I tried and what worked.

| Platform | My Experience | Recommendation |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| **LinkedIn** | Found my $4,000 job here. CTOs message you if your content is good. | Essential. Post weekly. |
| **Turing** | Applied 12 times. Rejected 11. Hired on the 12th. $30-70/hour range. | High bar. Worth it once you have experience. |
| **Andela** | Applied but never heard back. They focus on senior developers now. | Skip unless you have 3+ years. |
| **Upwork** | Got one $200 gig. Platform fees are brutal. Race to the bottom. | Avoid for developers. |
| **Twitter/X** | Built relationships that led to DMs and referrals. | Post daily. Engage genuinely. |
| **Dev.to** | Articles got 10,000+ views. Led to 2 job inquiries. | Cross-post your blog here. |

The pattern: inbound works better than outbound. When clients find you through your content, they are pre-sold. When you apply cold, you are one of 500 resumes.

## 7. The money reality: what $4,000 means in Lagos

Here is what nobody tells you about remote work from Nigeria.

**The good:**
- $4,000 is roughly ₦6.2 million at current rates
- That is 15x the average Lagos developer salary
- I can afford a generator, solar backup, and unlimited data
- I save 40% of my income because my cost of living is low

**The hard:**
- Getting paid is complicated. Wire transfers take 3-5 days. PayPal does not work for Nigerians. I use Wise and Payoneer.
- Taxes are confusing. I pay Nigerian taxes on foreign income. I hired an accountant for ₦50,000 a year.
- Internet is still unreliable. I have three backups: fiber, mobile data, and a coworking space membership.
- Timezone overlap means my meetings are at 2 PM or 7 PM. I rarely have morning calls.

<Callout type="tip" title="Payment setup for Nigerians">
Open a Wise account before you get your first client. It gives you a UK bank account number that European companies can pay into. Transfer to your Nigerian account takes 24 hours. Fees are 0.5%. This is the single most important financial tool for remote African developers.
</Callout>

## 8. What I would do differently

If I started over today, here is what I would change:

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Wasted 3 months on tutorial videos without building | Start with a real project on day one |
| Did not document my learning for 4 months | Start the blog immediately, even if it is bad |
| Applied to 50 jobs before I had a portfolio | Build 3 projects first, then apply to 5 targeted jobs |
| Undercharged my first client | Quote 2x what feels comfortable. You can always negotiate down. |
| Ignored LinkedIn for 6 months | Post weekly from day one. It is free marketing. |

The biggest mistake: I thought I needed to be ready before I started. I was wrong. You are never ready. You start, you learn, you improve.

## 9. Can you do this too?

Yes. But only if you meet these conditions:

- You can code for 4+ hours a day, 6 days a week, for at least 6 months
- You are willing to write publicly about what you learn, even when it is embarrassing
- You can survive 6-12 months without income from coding
- You have a real problem to solve or a real project to build
- You are patient enough to trust the process when nothing seems to work

If you cannot commit to those conditions, remote work is not the right path yet. Build locally first. Get a Nigerian job. Learn on someone else's dime. Then go remote when you have leverage.

<Callout type="info" title="The 6-month test">
If you can publish 24 blog posts, push 100 GitHub commits, and build 2 complete projects in the next 6 months, you will be ahead of 90% of developers applying for remote jobs. The bar is lower than you think.
</Callout>
